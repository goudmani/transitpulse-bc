The result

It beats every simpler predictor — by 10%

Mean absolute error, in seconds, on the same held-out arrivals. Lower is better. A model that cannot beat a lookup table has not earned the compute it cost.

The strongest baseline was not the obvious one. The project assumed persistence — "a bus four minutes late stays four minutes late" — would be the bar to clear. It wasn't. The historical median for that stop, at that hour, on that type of day is better: 127.3s against 130.2s. The registry gate was rewired to measure against whichever baseline actually wins, because a gate pointed at the wrong one would have passed a model a lookup table already beats.

Where it wins

The advantage is congestion, not the clock

Mean absolute error by hour of day, Vancouver local time. The gap between the blue line and the best grey one is the whole value of the model.

XGBoost historical median persistence published schedule

Between 09:00 and 19:00 the model beats the best baseline by 7–16%. Overnight it does not: with four buses on a route and empty roads, looking up what usually happens at that stop is as good as gradient boosting. That is the honest shape of the result, and it is the shape you would want — the model earns its keep exactly when a rider is standing in the rain caring about the answer.

Where it loses

Two of the twelve busiest routes beat it

On routes 049 and 100, persistence wins outright. Both are high-frequency trunk routes where buses bunch: when the vehicle in front is three minutes down, the simplest possible signal — how late this bus already is — carries almost everything, and the model's extra features add variance rather than information. Averages hide this. It is in the table because it is true.

What it learned

One feature does most of the work

Share of total split gain across 26 features.

delay_t_minus_15 — how late the bus already was fifteen minutes out — carries 44% of the gain. That is the same signal the persistence baseline uses. The model's advantage is not a better signal; it is knowing when to trust that one, using how far into the route the bus is, what the stop normally does at this hour, and what the buses ahead of it are doing.

Honest notes

What this does not show

It is fifteen trees

Training was configured for 800 boosting rounds. Early stopping halted at 64, with the best validation score at round 15. After that, training error kept falling while validation error rose — the model was learning one specific fortnight, not how buses behave.

One season

27 days in late summer. No snow, no ice, no winter darkness. The four weather features contribute almost nothing because there was almost no weather to learn from.

No live endpoint

The online feature store was switched off to cut cost, so a live endpoint would fall back to defaults for the four historical features and quietly serve worse predictions than the 114.3s reported here. A demo that lies is worse than no demo.

The tail is unpredictable

Half of predictions land within 72 seconds. The mean is 114 because of a minority of buses that blow out past ten minutes — bridge lifts, collisions, driver changeovers. Nothing in a schedule feed can see those coming.
